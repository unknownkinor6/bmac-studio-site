Loft Conversions

A loft is the cheapest floor area you will ever add: the roof, the walls and the foundations are already paid for. It is also the room where structure, fire safety and building regulations all land on the same decision, which is why lofts go wrong on paper long before anyone lifts a hammer.

01Will it work 02Order of decisions 03Four shapes 04The hidden parts 05Planning

Three checks, one afternoon

01 · Height

2.2 m

Measured from the top of the ceiling joist to the underside of the ridge. A rule of thumb, not a regulation — below it you are lowering ceilings underneath or raising the roof, and both change the budget category.

02 · Roof structure

Cut or truss

Older houses tend to have a cut roof — rafters and purlins, with usable space already inside. From roughly the mid 1960s, trussed rafters fill the void. They can go, but only once new steelwork carries the load.

03 · The stairs

Where?

The hardest of the three, and the one most often left until last.

The practice is led by an architect who is ARB registered and RIBA chartered. Plenty of people selling loft work locally are designers or installers rather than architects, and the roof is where that gap shows. More about the practice.

The order of decisions matters

On most projects the design comes first and the regulations are checked afterwards. A loft will not tolerate that order. The staircase position, the escape route, the steel layout and the head height are one problem, not four — and solving them separately produces a room you cannot legally use.

The four shapes a conversion takes

01

Rooflight

Windows in the plane of the roof, no change to its outline. Cheapest and quickest, and only an option where the height already works.

02

Dormer

A box built out from the rear slope, adding floor area and standing height. The usual answer for a terrace or semi — and the usual argument about how it looks from the garden.

03

Hip to gable

The sloping side is rebuilt as a vertical wall, turning a cramped triangle into a usable room. End terrace, semi and detached only.

04

Mansard

The roof is largely rebuilt at a steep pitch. The most space, the most cost, and the most planning sensitivity.

The parts nobody mentions until later

  • The staircase takes a room. It has to land somewhere, and that somewhere is usually a corner of the bedroom below. Decide early whether you are trading a small third bedroom for a large fourth.
  • Fire safety changes the whole house. Two storeys becoming three brings the stairwell into scope as a protected escape route: fire doors to habitable rooms, mains linked alarms, and often a rethink of an open plan hallway.
  • Head height over the stairs. Two metres of clear headroom is required, measured on the pitch line — which is where tight designs fail.
  • Party wall. Terraced and semi detached houses need notice served on neighbours before steel goes into a shared wall.

Most lofts do not need permission

A large share fall under permitted development, with a volume allowance of 40 cubic metres for a terraced house and 50 for a semi detached or detached one. Dormers on the roof slope facing the road are the common exception, along with conservation areas and flats.

The full picture, including what to do if you have already been refused, is on the planning permission page. If you are weighing going up against going out, that comparison is worth having before either is drawn.
