Building Conversions

Converting something that already exists is a planning question before it is a building question. Whether a garage, a barn or a shop can legally become somewhere to live is settled by change of use rules — and the answer arrives long before anyone thinks about kitchens.

01Change of use 02The building 03Garages & barns 04Building control

Change of use comes first

Every building has a use class. Moving between classes sometimes needs a full application, and sometimes runs through a lighter prior approval route instead.

Two of those routes matter around here. One allows suitable agricultural buildings to become dwellings. The other allows certain commercial premises to become homes. Both carry conditions on size, condition, how long the building has been vacant, and where it stands.

Those conditions get revised every few years, which is why no figures are published here. The only useful answer is one checked against the current rules for your specific building on the day you ask. How the planning routes work.

bMAC Studio is led by a registered architect. The word is protected by law, and the ARB holds the register that decides who may use it.

What the existing building will not do

The second question is whether the thing standing there can carry a conversion at all. They fail on the same handful of points.

  • Foundations. Garages and outbuildings were often built on far less than a house needs.
  • Damp. Many older structures have no damp proof course, and a floor slab laid without one cannot simply be carpeted over.
  • Floor level. Garage slabs usually sit below the house, so the new floor build-up eats head height you may not have.
  • Thermal performance. Walls built as a shed have to reach habitable standards, and the insulation has to go somewhere.

Garages and rural buildings

Garage conversions

The most common, and the most often underestimated. Filling the door opening, raising and insulating the floor, dealing with damp and getting a proper escape window into a new bedroom is most of a small building project.

Worth counting what you lose as well: off-street parking, and the only dry storage most houses have.

Barns and rural buildings

Kent has a lot of agricultural buildings and a steady trade in people buying one to convert. The building itself is rarely the hard part.

Whether it qualifies, whether it is sound enough to convert rather than rebuild, and what access and services it has are the questions that decide it. Get a view on those before you buy, not after.

What building control expects

A conversion is judged as a new dwelling in most respects: insulation, ventilation, escape windows, sound between dwellings where a building is split, and drainage that reaches somewhere legal. Existing walls and roofs usually need work to get there.

This is where detailed drawings pay for themselves, because a converted building has almost no standard details in it. Every junction is a decision. See what that looks like across 37 sheets.
